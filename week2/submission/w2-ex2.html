W2-Ex2 — Playing with the Embeddings Explorer

Demo week-02/demos/03-embeddings-explorer, running nomic-embed-text on local Ollama. Every sentence comes back as 768 numbers with norm = 1.0000 — a point on the unit sphere. “Similar” then just means “near”, and you can put a number on it.

1 — Meaning, not words

The winner at 0.865 is “The warehouse robot's top speed is 2.4 metres per second.” — and the only words the two share are the, warehouse and robot. It does not contain “quick”, and my query does not contain “speed” or “fast”. The cake recipe falls to the bottom at 0.331, below even the Acme Robotics line, which is about robots but not about speed. The ranking is tracking what the sentence is about, not which words it reuses. This is the case that matched what I expected.

2 — Two names, one geometry

Because the model returns unit-length vectors, the two measures are not independent: they are locked together by distance² = 2·(1 − cosine), so the distance is a strictly decreasing function of the cosine and whichever sentence has the highest cosine must have the lowest distance. There is only one quantity here — the angle between the two points. Cosine reports it directly; Euclidean distance reports the length of the chord that the angle subtends. Checking the identity against the numbers the demo printed: 

cos 0.865 → √(2·(1−0.865)) = 0.520 ≈ 0.519 shown
cos 0.331 → √(2·(1−0.331)) = 1.157 ≈ 1.156 shown
cos 0.955 → √(2·(1−0.955)) = 0.300 =  0.300 shown

3 — Trying to fool it

The negation was the case that surprised me. Adding the single word not reverses the truth of the sentence and moves the point almost nowhere — 0.955 is nearer than any genuine paraphrase I produced in task 1. That tells me an embedding captures topic — what a sentence is about, which words it lives among — and largely ignores polarity: “near” means “about the same thing”, not “says the same thing”. The language test points the same way. “Me gusta mucho esta pelicula” lands 0.776 from its Catalan twin but only 0.473 from its English translation — a gap of 0.303 for a sentence that means exactly the same thing — while an unrelated cake recipe sits at 0.405, just 0.068 further out than the translation. So the vector encodes which language the sentence is written in nearly as strongly as what it says; nomic-embed-text was trained mostly on English, and it shows. For RAG the consequence is direct — a manual saying “the warranty does not cover water damage” is exactly what gets retrieved for “is water damage covered?”, and the retriever cannot tell me which of the two it found. Retrieval finds the right paragraph; it does not read it.
